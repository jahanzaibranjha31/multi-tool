What Is BMI?

BMI stands for Body Mass Index. It is a numerical value derived from your height and weight that gives a rough estimate of your body fat level. The higher your BMI, the more weight you carry relative to your height — and vice versa.

The concept was developed in the 1830s by Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet, making it over 180 years old. It was originally called the "Quetelet Index" and was designed as a statistical tool for studying population trends — not for diagnosing individual health.

Today, the World Health Organization (WHO) uses BMI as a standard screening tool to classify weight status across populations globally. It is widely adopted by the medical community, governments, and health programs because it requires no special equipment and takes seconds to compute.

Quick Definition: BMI = your weight (in kilograms) ÷ your height squared (in meters). A BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is generally considered healthy for most adults.

Think of BMI as a starting point — a rough first signal, not a final diagnosis. It is best used alongside other measurements like waist circumference, blood pressure, blood glucose, and cholesterol.

How BMI Is Calculated

Calculating BMI requires just two pieces of information: your height and your weight. That's it. There is no blood draw, no imaging, no gym equipment needed.

BMI works the same way whether you're a 25-year-old or a 65-year-old — though, as we'll discuss later, the interpretation of the number can differ based on age, sex, and ethnicity.

BMI Formula Explained

Metric Formula (kg and cm)

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ [height (m)]²

Example: If you weigh 70 kg and are 1.75 m tall:

BMI = 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9

A result of 22.9 falls comfortably within the healthy range.

Imperial Formula (lbs and inches)

BMI = [weight (lbs) × 703] ÷ [height (inches)]²

Example: If you weigh 154 lbs and are 5'9" (69 inches) tall:

BMI = (154 × 703) ÷ (69 × 69) = 108,262 ÷ 4,761 = 22.7

Step-by-Step Calculation (Metric)

  1. Convert your height to meters if it's in centimetres (divide by 100). For example, 170 cm = 1.70 m.
  2. Square your height in metres: 1.70 × 1.70 = 2.89.
  3. Divide your weight in kilograms by that number: 65 ÷ 2.89 = 22.5.
  4. Your BMI is 22.5 — which is in the normal/healthy range.

Healthy BMI Range

The healthy BMI range for most adults is 18.5 to 24.9. Within this window, the risk of weight-related conditions — such as type 2 diabetes, heart disease, sleep apnea, and joint problems — is statistically at its lowest.

That said, "healthy" is not a single number. A BMI of 24.8 is not automatically healthier than one of 25.1. Think of these categories as zones, not precise thresholds. The goal is not to chase a specific BMI score — it is to support overall metabolic health through diet, movement, sleep, and stress management.

Note on ethnicity: Some research suggests that South Asian, East Asian, and Southeast Asian populations face elevated health risks at lower BMI values. The WHO acknowledges that adjusted BMI cut-offs may be more appropriate for certain ethnic groups. Always discuss your results with your doctor in the context of your full health picture.

Limitations of BMI

BMI is a useful tool, but it is far from a complete picture of your health. Understanding where it falls short is just as important as knowing what it measures.

Important: BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnostic test. It does not measure body fat directly, and a normal BMI does not guarantee good health — nor does a high BMI mean you are unhealthy in every way. Always speak with a qualified healthcare professional for a full health assessment.

BMI Does Not Account for Muscle Mass

Muscle is denser than fat. A highly muscular athlete — think professional rugby player or powerlifter — may register an "overweight" or even "obese" BMI despite having very low body fat. BMI cannot distinguish between a kilogram of muscle and a kilogram of fat.

BMI Ignores Body Fat Distribution

Where you carry your weight matters enormously. Visceral fat (the fat stored around your abdominal organs) is far more dangerous than subcutaneous fat (the fat just under your skin). Two people can share the exact same BMI yet have very different health risks based on where their fat is distributed.

BMI Varies by Ethnicity

Research published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and other bodies suggests that people of Asian descent may face elevated health risks at BMI levels below the standard "overweight" threshold of 25. Different population groups may need adjusted cut-off points.

BMI and Age

As people age, they tend to lose muscle mass and accumulate more body fat — even if their weight stays the same. An older adult may have a BMI in the "normal" range while carrying a significant amount of excess body fat.

BMI Is Not Designed for Children

Standard adult BMI cut-offs do not apply to children. For those under 18, BMI is interpreted using age- and sex-specific percentile charts developed by the CDC. More on this in the children's section below.

Other Measures Worth Considering

  • Waist circumference: A waist measurement above 88 cm (35 in) for women or 102 cm (40 in) for men suggests elevated abdominal fat risk.
  • Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR): Compares waist size to hip size; associated with cardiovascular risk.
  • Body fat percentage: Measured via DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing, or bioelectrical impedance; the most direct body composition measure.
  • Waist-to-height ratio: Some researchers consider this one of the most predictive single measures of cardiometabolic risk.

BMI for Men

The standard BMI formula and classification ranges are the same for men and women. A healthy BMI for adult men falls between 18.5 and 24.9.

However, men and women differ in body composition at the same BMI. Men typically have more muscle mass and less body fat than women at identical BMI values. This means that a man with a BMI of 27 may have a healthier body composition than a woman with the same BMI — though this is a broad generalisation and individual variation is significant.

Research suggests that men who carry excess weight around the abdomen (apple-shaped body distribution) are at particularly elevated risk for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.

A waist circumference greater than 102 cm (40 inches) for men is considered a risk factor for obesity-related conditions, regardless of BMI.

BMI for Women

Like men, women's healthy BMI range is 18.5 to 24.9 by the standard WHO classification. However, women naturally carry a higher percentage of body fat than men — typically 21–35% for a healthy adult woman versus 8–25% for a healthy adult man.

This means that at the same BMI, a woman will generally have a higher body fat percentage than a man. The BMI calculation itself does not adjust for this difference, which is one of the reasons some health professionals argue it is less accurate for women, particularly those who are very athletic.

For women, waist circumference is an especially important supplementary measure. A waist measurement above 88 cm (35 inches) is associated with elevated risk for heart disease and diabetes, regardless of total BMI.

Hormonal changes — particularly around menopause — can cause shifts in body composition that BMI does not capture. Women entering perimenopause or post-menopause may see their body fat redistribute even without significant weight gain.

BMI for Children and Teens

For children and adolescents aged 2 to 19, BMI is calculated using the same formula as adults — but it is interpreted very differently. Instead of fixed cut-off ranges, BMI for children is assessed using age- and sex-specific percentile charts developed by the CDC.

This is because children's body composition changes rapidly as they grow, and what is normal varies significantly by age and sex.

For example, a BMI of 22 in a 12-year-old boy might be in the 90th percentile (overweight), while the same BMI in a 17-year-old boy might be in the 60th percentile (healthy weight). Context — meaning age and sex — is everything for children.

Parents concerned about their child's weight should consult a paediatrician rather than relying solely on online BMI calculators. The CDC offers a dedicated BMI-for-age tool for children.

BMI During Pregnancy

Standard BMI calculators are not suitable for use during pregnancy. Weight gain during pregnancy is normal, necessary, and expected — and applying standard BMI categories to a pregnant woman would produce meaningless or misleading results.

Instead, healthcare providers use a woman's pre-pregnancy BMI to set personalised gestational weight gain targets. The Institute of Medicine (now the National Academy of Medicine) published guidelines outlining recommended weight gain ranges based on pre-pregnancy BMI:

Tips to Maintain a Healthy BMI

If your BMI is outside the healthy range, the good news is that even modest improvements in weight and lifestyle can have meaningful health benefits. Here are evidence-based strategies to help you move toward and maintain a healthy BMI.

1. Focus on Whole, Nutrient-Dense Foods

Build your meals around vegetables, legumes, lean proteins, whole grains, and healthy fats. These foods are satiating and nutrient-rich, making it easier to manage portion sizes without feeling deprived.

2. Move Your Body Every Day

The WHO recommends at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week for adults. This does not have to mean the gym — brisk walking, cycling, swimming, or dancing all count.

3. Build Muscle Mass

Resistance training (weightlifting, bodyweight exercises) helps you maintain and build lean muscle, which raises your resting metabolic rate. More muscle means your body burns more calories at rest.

4. Prioritise Sleep

Poor sleep disrupts hunger hormones (ghrelin and leptin), leading to increased appetite and cravings for high-calorie foods. Aim for 7–9 hours of quality sleep per night.

5. Manage Stress Mindfully

Chronic stress elevates cortisol levels, which is associated with abdominal fat accumulation. Practices like meditation, yoga, deep breathing, and spending time in nature can help manage stress effectively.

6. Stay Hydrated

Drinking enough water supports metabolism, reduces hunger signals that are sometimes confused with thirst, and improves physical performance. Most adults benefit from 2–3 litres of water per day.

7. Avoid Fad Diets

Extreme or crash diets may produce rapid short-term weight loss, but they are difficult to sustain and often cause muscle loss. Focus on consistent, sustainable changes you can maintain long-term.

8. Track Progress Without Obsessing

Check your BMI every few weeks rather than every day — weight naturally fluctuates by 1–2 kg over the course of a day based on hydration, food, and hormones. Use our BMI Calculator periodically to track meaningful trends over time.

Frequently Asked Questions About BMI

What is a normal BMI for adults?

A normal BMI for adults is between 18.5 and 24.9. This range is associated with the lowest statistical risk of weight-related health problems. Values below 18.5 indicate underweight, while values of 25 or above indicate overweight or obesity.

How is BMI calculated?

In metric units: BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²). In imperial units: BMI = (weight in lbs × 703) ÷ height² (inches²). You can skip the maths entirely by using our free BMI Calculator.

Is BMI accurate for everyone?

No. BMI is a useful population-level screening tool, but it has real limitations for individuals. It does not account for muscle mass, bone density, age, sex, or ethnicity. Athletes may have a misleadingly high BMI, while older adults may have a normal BMI despite excess body fat. Always use it alongside other health metrics.

What is a healthy BMI for women?

The healthy BMI range for women is 18.5 to 24.9, the same as for men by standard classification. However, women naturally carry more body fat at the same BMI value as men, so waist circumference and body fat percentage are also useful supplementary measures for women.

Can I use a BMI calculator during pregnancy?

Standard BMI calculators are not designed for use during pregnancy because normal weight gain distorts results. Healthcare providers use your pre-pregnancy BMI to recommend appropriate gestational weight gain targets. Always consult your obstetrician or midwife for pregnancy weight guidance.

What BMI is considered obese?

A BMI of 30.0 or higher is classified as obese. Class I obesity is 30.0–34.9, Class II is 35.0–39.9, and Class III (severe obesity) is 40.0 and above. Each class carries progressively higher health risks.

How do I lower my BMI?

To lower your BMI, combine regular physical activity with a nutritious, calorie-appropriate diet. Sustainable weight loss of 0.5–1 kg (1–2 lbs) per week is generally recommended by health experts. Building muscle through resistance training also improves body composition even if BMI changes slowly.

Does a normal BMI mean I am healthy?

Not necessarily. A normal BMI reduces your statistical risk for many weight-related conditions, but it does not guarantee overall health. Factors like blood pressure, blood glucose, cholesterol levels, sleep quality, stress, and physical fitness all matter. BMI is one piece of a much larger picture.

What is the BMI formula for children?

For children aged 2–19, BMI is calculated using the same formula as adults, but the result is plotted on age- and sex-specific percentile charts rather than fixed categories. For example, a BMI in the 85th–94th percentile for age and sex is classified as overweight, while the 95th percentile or above is classified as obese.

How often should I check my BMI?

For most adults, checking BMI once a month is more than sufficient. Weight fluctuates naturally day to day based on hydration, food, and hormones, so daily checks are not useful or recommended. If you are actively working on weight management, monthly tracking lets you observe meaningful trends over time.

Conclusion

BMI is a powerful, simple starting point for understanding your body weight relative to your height. It has been used by health professionals and researchers for decades precisely because it is fast, free, and broadly predictive of health risks at the population level.

But it is a starting point — not a finish line. A BMI number tells you something real, but it cannot tell you everything. Muscle mass, fat distribution, age, ethnicity, and lifestyle all contribute to your true health picture in ways a simple formula cannot capture.

The most useful thing you can do with your BMI result is use it to start a conversation — with your doctor, with a registered dietitian, or just with yourself. Are you moving enough? Eating well? Sleeping adequately? Managing stress?

If you haven't already, take 30 seconds right now and check your BMI. Knowledge is the first step toward positive change.
