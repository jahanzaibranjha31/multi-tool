№ 02 — Field Playbook

A free keyword density checker is only half the answer.

The other half is knowing what number to expect for the page you're actually writing. A 1,800-word tutorial, a 60-word product description, and a landing page headline don't play by the same rules — this playbook gives you a target for each.

10 min read· Updated July 2026· United States edition

Why a free tool is enough

The math behind keyword density has exactly one formula: occurrences of a phrase, divided by total word count, times 100. That's a spreadsheet-level calculation. A free keyword density checker and a paid one run the identical arithmetic — what paid tools usually add on top is competitor comparisons, saved history, or bulk uploads, not a more accurate number.

For a single blogger, a solo SEO writer, or a small store owner checking product copy, a free checker gets the job done. Where people get stuck isn't the tool — it's not knowing what number they should be aiming for once the checker gives them one.

Three page types, three different targets

Density isn't one-size-fits-all because word count and repetition patterns aren't one-size-fits-all. Here's how the same primary keyword tends to behave across three common page types.

~1,600 typical word count

0.4% – 1.5% typical density

Long-form content spreads a keyword across an introduction, several sections, and a conclusion, so it can cover the topic thoroughly without repeating the exact phrase often. Synonyms and sub-questions do most of the relevance work here.

~80–150 typical word count

2% – 5% typical density

Short copy plus a repeated product name in the title, a bullet, and the description pushes density up fast — often past what would look like stuffing on a blog post, yet still reads naturally at this length.

~300–600 typical word count

1.5% – 3.5% typical density

Headlines, subheads, and calls-to-action are built to repeat the same core promise on purpose. A little repetition here is a feature of persuasive writing, not a red flag.

Walkthrough: a blog post or tutorial

Say you're writing a 1,500-word how-to guide targeting "meal prep containers." Paste the finished draft into a checker before you publish and look for three things:

  1. The exact phrase sits under 1.5%. Above that in a long article usually means it's been forced into sentences where a pronoun or synonym would read better.
  2. Related terms appear too. "Food storage," "glass containers," "airtight lids" — a checker that only reports one phrase will miss whether you've actually covered the topic's vocabulary.
  3. Headings aren't repeating the identical phrase verbatim. If four out of six subheadings contain the same exact words, that's a stuffing pattern hiding in your structure, not your paragraphs.

Walkthrough: a product description

Product copy is short by nature, which changes how you should read a density number. A 100-word description that mentions "stainless steel water bottle" three times sits at roughly 3% — high by blog-post standards, but often unavoidable when the phrase needs to appear in the title, a spec line, and the closing sentence.

The better check for product pages: read the description out loud. If the repeated phrase still sounds like normal spoken language and not a chant, the number is fine regardless of what a blog-length benchmark would suggest.

Walkthrough: a landing page

Landing pages are written to persuade, not to explain — so some repetition of the core offer is intentional. A headline, a supporting subheadline, a benefits list, and a call-to-action button might all legitimately use a version of the same phrase, like "free shipping" or "24/7 support."

The failure mode to watch for isn't the density number itself — it's using the identical exact phrase in every instance instead of varying it slightly ("free shipping" → "ships free" → "no shipping cost"). That variation keeps a naturally higher density from reading as robotic.

Mistakes specific to product and landing pages

  • Copy-pasted boilerplate across a catalog. The same intro sentence on fifty product pages reads as duplicate content, and checking density won't catch that — a separate content audit will.
  • Stuffing the meta title to match the H1 exactly. Titles and meta descriptions are separate fields; repeating the identical string in both wastes a chance to use a variant phrase.
  • Treating every SKU the same. A flagship product page can support more depth and a lower density than a simple variant page (same item, different color) — don't force both to hit an identical number.
  • Ignoring image alt text. On product pages especially, alt text is often the most repetitive spot on the page and rarely gets reread before publishing.

Frequently asked questions

Are free keyword density checkers accurate?

Yes. The calculation itself — occurrences divided by total word count — is simple math, so a free checker and a paid one will return the same number for the same text. Paid tools add extras like competitor comparisons, not a more accurate formula.

Should product pages have the same keyword density as blog posts?

No. Product pages are short and specification-heavy, so the same keyword often appears in the title, a bullet, and the description, which naturally pushes density higher than a 1,500-word blog post covering the same term once every few paragraphs.

What's a realistic density target for a landing page?

Landing pages are typically shorter and more repetitive by design, since a headline, subheading, and call-to-action often reuse the same core phrase. A density between 1.5% and 3.5% is common and usually still reads naturally at that length.

Can I check keyword density for a whole product catalog at once?

Most free checkers work on one page of text at a time. For a catalog, run each product description separately, or paste a handful together if you're checking for repeated boilerplate phrasing across listings.
