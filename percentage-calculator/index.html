      Percentage Calculator: The Complete Guide

      What Is a Percentage?

      A percentage is a way of expressing a number as a fraction of 100. The word itself comes from the Latin per centum, meaning "by the hundred." When you see 25%, it literally means 25 out of every 100 — the same value as the fraction 25/100 or the decimal 0.25.

      Percentages are useful precisely because they standardize comparisons. Saying "18 out of 40 students passed" is harder to compare at a glance than saying "45% of students passed." Converting any ratio into a percentage puts it on the same 0–100 scale as every other percentage, which is why percentages show up everywhere from report cards to receipts to stock market tickers.

      The Percentage Formula

      Nearly every percentage problem is a variation of one core relationship:

      Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100

      From that single formula, you can rearrange the algebra to solve for any of the three variables, depending on which two you already know:

      Percentage formula variations
      You knowYou wantFormula
      Percent & WholePartPart = (Percent ÷ 100) × Whole
      Part & WholePercentPercent = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100
      Part & PercentWholeWhole = Part ÷ (Percent ÷ 100)

      How to Calculate a Percentage Step by Step

      To find what X% of a number Y is, without a calculator:

      1. Convert the percentage to a decimal by dividing it by 100 (20% becomes 0.20).
      2. Multiply that decimal by the number you're taking the percentage of.
      3. The result is your answer, already in the same units as the original number.

      For example, 20% of 150 is calculated as 0.20 × 150 = 30. The tool above performs this instantly and also shows the reverse calculations — working out what percent one number is of another, or what the original number was before a percentage was applied.

      Percentage Increase Explained

      A percentage increase describes how much a value has grown, relative to where it started. The formula is:

      Percentage Increase = ((New − Original) ÷ Original) × 100

      If a salary rises from $40,000 to $45,000, the increase is (5,000 ÷ 40,000) × 100 = 12.5%. The Percentage Increase calculator above works in the opposite direction too: give it an original value and a percentage, and it tells you the new value directly — useful for projecting raises, price hikes, or population growth.

      Percentage Decrease Explained

      A percentage decrease works the same way in reverse:

      Percentage Decrease = ((Original − New) ÷ Original) × 100

      If a laptop's price drops from $1,000 to $750, the decrease is (250 ÷ 1,000) × 100 = 25%. This is closely related to, but not identical to, a discount calculation — see the discount section below for how retailers apply this in practice.

      How to Calculate a Discount

      Discounts are one of the most common real-world uses of percentages. To calculate a discounted price:

      1. Multiply the original price by the discount percentage (as a decimal) to find the amount saved.
      2. Subtract that amount from the original price to get the final price.

      A $80 jacket with a 30% discount saves you 0.30 × $80 = $24, bringing the final price to $56. The Discount calculator above shows both numbers instantly, along with a step-by-step breakdown.

      Profit Margin vs. Markup

      These two terms are frequently confused because they describe the same profit using a different base number.

      Profit margin versus markup comparison
      TermBaseFormula
      Profit MarginSelling Price(Profit ÷ Selling Price) × 100
      MarkupCost Price(Profit ÷ Cost Price) × 100

      A product that costs $50 and sells for $75 has a $25 profit. That's a 33.3% margin ($25 ÷ $75) but a 50% markup ($25 ÷ $50) — the same dollars, two different percentages. Confusing the two is one of the most common pricing mistakes small businesses make.

      Common Percentage Mistakes to Avoid

      • Confusing percentage points with percent change. Going from 20% to 25% is a 5 percentage-point increase, but a 25% relative increase (5 ÷ 20).
      • Using the wrong base number. A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return you to the original value, because the second calculation uses a different (larger) base.
      • Mixing up margin and markup. As shown above, these produce different percentages from the same numbers.
      • Rounding too early. Round only your final answer, not the intermediate steps, to avoid compounding small errors.
      • Forgetting to convert the percent to a decimal before multiplying, which inflates results by a factor of 100.

      Best Practices for Accurate Percentage Calculations

      • Double-check which number is the "whole" (the denominator) before dividing — it's the most common source of errors.
      • Keep at least two decimal places during intermediate steps, and round only the final answer.
      • For business pricing, decide upfront whether you're working from cost (markup) or price (margin), and stay consistent.
      • When comparing two percentages, check whether they share the same base — percentages of different wholes aren't directly comparable.
      • Use the step-by-step explanation in each calculator above to verify your own manual math before submitting a report or invoice.

      Frequently Asked Questions

      What is the formula for calculating a percentage?

      To find what percentage one number is of another, divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100: Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100.

      How do I calculate a percentage increase?

      Subtract the original value from the new value, divide the result by the original value, then multiply by 100: Percentage Increase = ((New − Original) ÷ Original) × 100.

      How do I calculate a percentage decrease?

      Subtract the new value from the original value, divide the result by the original value, then multiply by 100: Percentage Decrease = ((Original − New) ÷ Original) × 100.

      What is the difference between percentage change and percentage difference?

      Percentage change compares a new value to an original value and can be positive or negative. Percentage difference compares two values with no designated starting point, using their average as the base.

      How is profit margin different from markup?

      Profit margin divides profit by the selling price, while markup divides profit by the cost price. The two percentages describe the same profit from different baselines, so margin is always lower than markup on the same sale.
